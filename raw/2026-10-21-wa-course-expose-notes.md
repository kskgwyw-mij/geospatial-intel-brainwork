## From topic to research question (slide deck 1a)
Four levels: **Thema** (research area) → **Problem** (something in that area that needs a solution)
→ **Problembereich** (the context in which the problem exists) → **Forschungsfrage** (a concrete
question within that context). Slide example: AI in medicine → lack of explainability of AI diagnoses
→ doctors do not trust opaque diagnoses → which explainable-AI methods improve doctors' trust.
- A good research question must take the current state of research into account; a literature search
  is required, and whether it fills a gap must be judged by the student together with the supervisor.
- Suggested AI use: a "Socratic dialogue" with a chatbot about one's own brainstorm, to sharpen the
  question. Better still: develop and fix the question with the supervisor or a real expert.
- Topic finding is mostly personal (interests, prior projects, supervisors' expertise, career); AI tools
  help mainly for overviews of research fields (ResearchRabbit, Open Knowledge Maps, Keenious, STORM,
  Litmaps, Consensus).

## Good scientific practice with AI (slide deck 1a)
- Relevant rules: ASPO 2.8 (deception, fraud), the HOK master-thesis guide (Leitfaden), the HOK AI guideline.
- The author must be able to explain and defend every theoretical and methodological decision and conclusion.
- Usage levels, from worst to best: unreflected (AI replaces thinking — violates good scientific practice),
  relief (outsourcing low-level subtasks such as language editing), support (explaining terms, outlines,
  first-draft suggestions), extension (AI as a "thinking tutor": feedback, alternative versions, literature search).
- Transparency: document the procedure and name the AI tools used and what they were used for; more
  detailed documentation is safer. New: AI checks of final theses.
- AI-generated text is questionable as a citable source; direct adoption is not allowed under exam law.
- PAIR framework for choosing tools: problem formulation, AI-tool selection, interaction, reflection.

## Work phases and AI (slide deck 1b)
- Outline: concise, a clear common thread, every section contributes to answering the research question.
- Literature search tools by starting point: research question (Elicit, Consensus, ORKG Ask), article
  (ResearchRabbit, Keenious), prompt (Undermind), keywords (Open Knowledge Maps). Weaknesses: mostly
  English, open-access only, journals only, no quality check, misreporting, illusion of completeness.
- Reading: uploading copyrighted texts to AI reading tools (e.g. ChatPDF) is legally problematic; own reading is essential.
- Data collection and analysis: the lecturer strongly advises **against** using AI for data-related work.
- Drafting: AI only for starting points, not finished text; writing is iterative.
- Challenges listed: technical, ethical, political, ecological, data protection, cognitive (deskilling), epistemic (hallucination, illusion of understanding).

## Exposé assignment (slide deck 1b, quoted headings)
- Task: write an exposé for the master thesis, possibly with AI tools; document exactly which tools were
  used for which tasks.
- First submission for the WA course via Moodle by **23.10.2026** (a duplicate slide says 23.10.2025 — likely an old copy).
- Two separate PDFs:
  1. the exposé — **the author's name must not appear in the document**;
  2. documentation of the tools used plus an estimate in % of AI use (100 % = fully AI-made, 0 % = no AI).
     **"Über 50% sind nicht erlaubt!"** (more than 50 % is not allowed).
- Required exposé structure ("Gliederung Exposé"):
  1. Arbeitstitel
  2. Ausgangssituation und Problemstellung
  3. Relevanz
  4. Zielsetzung und Ergebnisse
  5. Vorgehensweise und Methoden
  6. Literatur
- Working title: simple (no jargon or abbreviations), self-explanatory to non-experts, to the point
  (interest drops beyond about 8–12 words; ideally one line), makes the reader curious without giving away results.
- Relevance: theoretical (a knowledge gap) and practical (a practical problem); the reader must see the added value.
- Goals and results: precise scientific goals, practical goals, expected results (e.g. prototype,
  recommendations, theoretical results), the derived research question. A more precise goal makes the work easier.
- Approach and methods: which theoretical framework, which steps, which methods per step.
- Literature: prior work (for the starting situation), literature on the problem's relevance, existing
  results, references for the planned or similar methods.
